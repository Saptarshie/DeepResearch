### Source: [Government bailouts of private industry | Politics and Government | Research Starters | EBSCO Research](https://www.ebsco.com/research-starters/politics-and-government/government-bailouts-private-industry)

The U.S. government has a long history of providing bailouts during economic crises. Early interventions include Alexander Hamilton's response to the Panic of 1792, where the government borrowed from banks to buy back failing bonds, and the rescue of banks during the Panic of 1907. During the Great Depression, the Home Owners' Loan Corporation refinanced defaulted mortgages, ultimately helping 80% of borrowers and turning a small profit. The savings-and-loan crisis of the late 1980s–early 1990s cost taxpayers an estimated $124 billion via the Resolution Trust Corporation. In the 21st century, major bailouts include: $15 billion for the airline industry after the September 11 attacks (which later yielded a $140 million profit for the government); the 2008 financial sector bailout, where the Treasury lent to JPMorgan to buy Bear Stearns, injected $100 billion each into Freddie Mac and Fannie Mae, and took over AIG with up to $85 billion in loans; and nearly $60 billion for General Motors and Chrysler in 2009. These interventions were justified as necessary to prevent systemic economic collapse and protect jobs, but they remain controversial due to taxpayer costs and moral hazard concerns.

In October 2025, the U.S. Treasury deployed the Exchange Stabilization Fund (ESF) to provide a $20 billion bailout to Argentina. The package included a currency swap line via the Central Bank, a purchase of Argentina’s USD-denominated bonds, and a standby credit line. This was the second-largest use of the ESF for a foreign country in history. The bailout was authorized by Treasury Secretary Scott Bessent and was openly framed by President Trump as strengthening ideological ties with Argentine President Javier Milei. The Congressional Research Service later noted that the action 'renewed debates about the ESF’s function and purpose.'
