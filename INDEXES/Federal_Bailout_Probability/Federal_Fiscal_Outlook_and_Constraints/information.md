### Source: [US debt to break WWII record by 2030, CBO projects in new budget report | Fox Business](https://www.foxbusiness.com/politics/us-debt-set-crush-world-war-ii-record-annual-deficits-explode-3t-within-decade)

The Congressional Budget Office (CBO) released a budget and economic outlook spanning 2026–2036, projecting that the U.S. national debt will surpass its World War II-era record by 2030. Key projections include:

- **Federal budget deficits**: estimated $1.9 trillion in fiscal year 2026, rising to $3.1 trillion by 2036.
- **Gross federal debt**: $39.4 trillion at end of FY2026, rising to $63 trillion by 2036.
- **Debt held by the public**: from $32 trillion to $56 trillion; as a share of GDP, it reaches **108% in 2030** (exceeding the previous record of 106% in 1946) and **120% by 2036**.
- **Net interest costs**: surge from over $1 trillion (3.3% of GDP) in FY2026 to more than $2.1 trillion (4.6% of GDP) in 2036, accounting for nearly 14% of federal spending this year and rising to 19% by 2036.

The CBO warned that debt held by the public is expected to grow faster than GDP, which could slow economic growth, reduce private investment, and increase vulnerability to higher interest rates. The risk of a fiscal crisis—a loss of investor confidence in U.S. government debt—would rise, potentially causing abrupt interest rate increases and economic disruptions. Higher inflation expectations could erode the dollar's status as the dominant international reserve currency. Moreover, lawmakers may feel constrained in using fiscal policy to respond to unforeseen events.

Commentators from the Peter G. Peterson Foundation and the Committee for a Responsible Federal Budget called the outlook "an urgent warning" and stressed that stabilizing the debt is essential to avoid higher interest costs and economic instability. These fiscal constraints directly bear on the federal government’s capacity to provide bailouts to state and local entities, as rising mandatory spending on interest leaves less room for discretionary interventions.

The Congressional Research Service report on the Exchange Stabilization Fund (ESF) outlined several policy options for Congress to constrain future bailouts: capping the ESF’s size, limiting how it can be used, or requiring congressional approval when the Treasury Secretary initiates interventions. It also recommended new reporting requirements to improve the flow of information to Congress. The ESF was originally created in 1934 to maintain the fixed-dollar exchange rate; after the U.S. abandoned the gold standard in 1971, its use was expanded to support foreign governments, certain IMF transactions, and to stabilize U.S. financial markets. The controversy surrounding the Argentina bailout highlights the tension between executive flexibility and legislative oversight. In the context of a deteriorating federal fiscal position (CBO projects debt reaching 108% of GDP by 2030, with deficits of $3 trillion and interest costs of $2.1 trillion annually by 2036), any large-scale federal bailout—whether for foreign nations or domestic pension systems—will face heightened scrutiny and potential statutory limits, further reducing the probability of such intervention before 2030.
