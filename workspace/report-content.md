

## Pension Crisis Overview

The fiscal health of state and local public pension systems in the United States remains precarious, despite a modest improvement in the most recent fiscal year. As of fiscal year 2024, these systems hold an estimated $1.48 trillion in unfunded liabilities—the gap between promised benefits and the assets set aside to pay them—according to the Reason Foundation’s annual report. State-administered plans account for $1.29 trillion of this total, while local government plans add another $187 billion.[^1] The median funded ratio stands at just 78%, meaning that governments have saved, on average, only 78 cents for every dollar of vested benefits. This persistent shortfall exposes millions of public workers and retirees to significant risk and places growing pressure on state and local budgets.

The recent decline in the headline unfunded-liability figure—from $1.62 trillion in 2023 to $1.48 trillion in 2024—was driven largely by higher‑than‑expected investment returns during the year. However, this does not signal a structural repair; rather, it underscores how heavily pension solvency depends on favorable market conditions. Historical precedent illustrates the speed at which conditions can deteriorate: between 2007 and 2010, during the Great Recession, aggregate unfunded liabilities exploded by more than $1.15 trillion, an increase of over 800%.[^2] Should another severe economic downturn occur, stress tests project that unfunded liabilities could balloon to $2.74 trillion by 2026, dragging the average funded ratio down to 63%.[^3] Even a milder shock—a 20% decline in equity markets—would leave many plans critically underfunded.

Several structural factors contribute to this vulnerability. Many plans continue to rely on actuarial assumptions that a wide range of economists regard as optimistic, particularly assumed annual investment returns in the 7‑8% range. Inadequate employer contributions, often the result of political pressure to minimize near‑term budget impacts, cause the funding gap to widen year after year. Moreover, in pursuit of higher returns, public pension funds have shifted an increasing share of their portfolios into alternative assets—private equity, hedge funds, real estate, and infrastructure—whose illiquidity and volatility can amplify losses during market dislocations. Together, these elements create a chronic mismatch between liabilities (which are constitutionally or contractually protected in many states) and the resources available to meet them.

The sheer scale of the pension debt, combined with its systemic linkages to municipal bond markets, public‑sector employment, and the broader economy, raises the question of whether the federal government might eventually be compelled to intervene. While no formal bailout mechanism exists today, historical precedents—from the savings and loan crisis to the Troubled Asset Relief Program—show that Washington has acted when systemic financial stability was at stake. The following sections examine the factors that could influence the probability of a federal bailout occurring before 2030.

## Historical Precedents of Federal Bailouts

The prospect of a federal rescue for ailing public pension systems is not without precedent. Throughout its history, the U.S. government has repeatedly intervened in financial and industrial crises to prevent economic collapse, protect jobs, and stabilize markets. These episodes provide a framework for understanding when and how Washington might respond to a pension funding emergency—though the scale and political context of a $1.48 trillion liability present unique challenges.

### From the Panic of 1792 to the Great Depression

Federal bailouts are almost as old as the Republic. In the Panic of 1792, Treasury Secretary Alexander Hamilton arranged for the government to borrow from banks in order to purchase depreciated federal bonds, quelling a market panic. More than a century later, the Panic of 1907 prompted the rescue of major trust companies through coordinated private and government action. The Great Depression brought a more direct intervention: the Home Owners’ Loan Corporation, created in 1933, refinanced over a million defaulted mortgages, ultimately assisting 80 percent of eligible borrowers and even generating a modest profit for the Treasury[^1]. These early actions established the principle that the federal government could and would act as a backstop in times of severe distress.

### The Savings-and-Loan Crisis

The first modern, large-scale bailout occurred during the savings-and-loan (S&L) crisis of the late 1980s and early 1990s. Hundreds of S&L institutions failed under the weight of bad real estate loans and interest-rate mismatches. The government’s response—chiefly through the Resolution Trust Corporation—cost taxpayers an estimated $124 billion by the time the cleanup was complete[^1]. The S&L crisis hardened public attitudes against bailouts and introduced the concept of moral hazard into the political lexicon, but it also demonstrated that Congress was willing to appropriate massive sums to contain systemic damage to the financial system.

### The 21st Century Mega-Bailouts

The opening decades of the 21st century saw federal interventions multiply in size and scope. Following the September 11, 2001, terrorist attacks, Washington provided $15 billion in loan guarantees and direct aid to the airline industry—a package that ultimately yielded a $140 million profit for the government. Far larger were the actions taken during the 2008 global financial crisis. The Troubled Asset Relief Program (TARP) authorized up to $700 billion to stabilize banks; the Treasury injected $100 billion each into government-sponsored enterprises Fannie Mae and Freddie Mac; and the Federal Reserve and Treasury extended an $85 billion credit line to insurer American International Group (AIG). In 2009, the auto industry received nearly $60 billion to keep General Motors and Chrysler afloat[^1].

Each of these bailouts was justified on the grounds of systemic risk: the failure of a major institution or sector, it was argued, would ripple through the broader economy, destroying millions of jobs and deepening the recession. They were fiercely contested, however, with critics pointing to the costs to taxpayers, the rewarding of risky behavior, and the entrenchment of “too big to fail” expectations.

### A New Frontier: The 2025 Argentina Bailout

A recent episode, while not directly related to domestic pensions, illustrates the expanding toolkit and willingness of the Treasury to deploy extraordinary financial support. In October 2025, the U.S. Treasury used the Exchange Stabilization Fund (ESF) to provide a $20 billion bailout to Argentina. The package—which included a currency swap line, purchase of Argentine bonds, and a standby credit line—was the second-largest use of the ESF for a foreign country in history. Authorized by Treasury Secretary Scott Bessent and publicly framed by President Trump as strengthening ties with Argentine President Javier Milei, the intervention prompted the Congressional Research Service to note that it had “renewed debates about the ESF’s function and purpose”[^2]. The Argentina bailout underscores that, under a sympathetic administration, the federal government is capable of deploying creative mechanisms to rescue entities it deems important—even if those entities are not domestic U.S. institutions.

### Implications for a State and Local Pension Bailout

The historical record sends a dual message. On one side, there is clear evidence that when the perceived threat to the economy is severe enough, Washington will override moral-hazard objections and commit hundreds of billions of dollars to a rescue. The S&L crisis, the 2008 financial system interventions, and the auto bailout all occurred despite significant public opposition. On the other side, every past bailout was tailored to a specific industry or segment of the financial system; there is no precedent for a direct federal takeover of state and local pension obligations, which are legally distinct and involve a web of constitutional, contractual, and political complexities. Moreover, the sheer magnitude of the unfunded liability—$1.48 trillion under current assumptions, rising to as much as $2.74 trillion in a recession scenario—dwarfs all previous interventions combined.

The next sections evaluate how the federal government’s own deteriorating fiscal position and the evolving political landscape further shape the probability of a bailout before 2030.

## Risk Factors Driving the Probability of a Federal Bailout

The lessons of past bailouts underscore that federal intervention typically occurs when a confluence of risk factors makes inaction more perilous than action. In the case of public pensions, several key vulnerabilities are building that could tip the scales toward a federal rescue.

- **Chronic underperformance.** Over the past 24 years, public pension funds have earned an average annual return of 6.62%, consistently below their assumed rates (which averaged 6.87% in 2024). This shortfall is a primary driver of unfunded liabilities.
- **Aggressive assumptions.** Many plans continue to set optimistic assumed rates of return. As pension expert Josh McGee notes, “if they miss their 7% return by just a little bit it will not be cost neutral—it will have a significant, meaningful cost to the city.” A 1% miss can generate substantial new debt.
- **Contribution inadequacy.** In 2024, 54% of all employer contributions went to paying down legacy debt rather than funding new benefits, allowing debt to compound.
- **Risky asset allocation.** Pension portfolios have shifted heavily toward alternative assets (private equity, hedge funds, real estate) seeking higher returns, which increases volatility and opacity.
- **Dangerous feedback loop.** Unrealistic return assumptions → underfunding → pressure to take on more investment risk → greater vulnerability to market shocks → higher unfunded liabilities → even more unrealistic assumptions.
- **Demographic pressures.** An aging population increases benefit payouts while the active-to-retiree ratio declines, straining the funding model.

Together, these factors create a scenario in which a severe economic downturn could overwhelm state and local budgets, making a federal rescue politically and economically compelling.

### Political and Public Sentiment

Political and public sentiment is a decisive but volatile factor in the probability of a federal pension bailout. Historical rescue packages—such as the Troubled Asset Relief Program (TARP) and the 2008–2009 auto industry bailout—provoked fierce public backlash, as taxpayers viewed the interventions as rewards for institutional mismanagement and excessive risk‑taking.[^1] That legacy continues to color the political landscape, making large‑scale transfers to state and local pension systems an especially difficult proposition.

Several current dynamics are intensifying the tension:
- **Erosion of hard‑won reforms**: In California, legislative efforts such as Assembly Bill 569 threaten to reverse key cost‑control measures of the 2013 PEPRA framework, potentially adding billions in future liabilities and signaling to other states that fiscal discipline can be politically dismantled.
- **Misuse of pension infrastructure**: Colorado’s proposal to route police‑training funding through the Public Employees’ Retirement Association (PERA) introduces non‑pension risks into an already stressed plan, undermining the integrity of the trust fund and increasing the likelihood of future distress.
- **Echoes of discipline**: Former California Governor Jerry Brown has warned that “the great danger of pensions is that risk comes later when the current lawmakers and advocates are no longer around,” a sentiment reinforced by editorial boards such as the *New York Daily News*, which urges a halt to short‑term pandering on pension issues.
- **Demands for transparency**: Investors, watchdog groups, and rating agencies are pressing for clearer fee disclosures and more realistic accounting assumptions. While greater transparency could restrain reckless behavior, it might simultaneously expose deeper insolvency, accelerating the arrival of a crisis.

Public tolerance for a federal bailout of public‑employee benefits remains low. State and local government workers are often perceived as enjoying retirement benefits far more generous than those in the private sector, making the use of general taxpayer funds to shore up those promises politically toxic. Nonetheless, history suggests that if a pension meltdown threatens core public services—such as police, fire, or basic municipal operations—the political calculus can shift rapidly. The 2023 bailout of Argentina’s financial markets, for which the Treasury used the Exchange Stabilization Fund, offers a recent object lesson. The decision triggered a sharp congressional backlash: Congressman David Scott and eleven co‑signers labeled it “reckless,” while Senators Elizabeth Warren and Bernie Sanders introduced the *No Argentina Bailout Act* to block future unilateral rescues. Senator Sanders explicitly contrasted the “$20 billion to bail out the right‑wing regime of Argentina” with simultaneous domestic spending cuts. A Congressional Research Service report further noted that some members opposed the move because it appeared “to be an effort to unduly influence Argentina’s democratic elections.” This episode illustrates both a growing partisan divide over executive‑led bailouts and the increasing willingness of Congress to erect legislative roadblocks.

Conversely, the deteriorating federal fiscal position—with the Congressional Budget Office projecting debt to reach 108% of GDP by 2030 and interest costs surpassing $2 trillion annually—is amplifying public anxiety about adding new obligations. The combination of populist skepticism, reform backsliding, and congressional resistance means that, absent an acute systemic threat, the political will for a broad federal pension bailout is likely to be severely constrained.

## Federal Fiscal Outlook and Constraints

The probability of a federal bailout for state and local public pension systems cannot be assessed in isolation; it must be weighed against the federal government’s own rapidly deteriorating fiscal position. The Congressional Budget Office’s (CBO) latest long-term budget outlook paints a stark picture of rising debt, widening deficits, and mounting interest costs that will increasingly constrain discretionary spending. These dynamics represent a powerful headwind against any large-scale, non-emergency federal intervention before 2030.

### A Precarious Trajectory

According to the CBO’s 2026–2036 projections, federal deficits are expected to swell from $1.9 trillion in fiscal year 2026 to $3.1 trillion by 2036.[^1] Gross federal debt, already $39.4 trillion at the end of 2026, would climb to $63 trillion, while debt held by the public would surge from $32 trillion to $56 trillion. Most alarming is the debt-to-GDP ratio: by 2030, debt held by the public is forecast to reach 108% of GDP, surpassing the World War II-era record of 106% set in 1946. By 2036, that ratio climbs to 120%, a level unprecedented in U.S. history.

The primary driver of this fiscal erosion is the escalating cost of interest on the national debt. Net interest outlays are projected to more than double from over $1 trillion (3.3% of GDP) in 2026 to more than $2.1 trillion (4.6% of GDP) in 2036. In spending-share terms, interest payments will absorb nearly 14 cents of every federal dollar this year and 19 cents by the end of the decade. This rapid growth in mandatory, non-discretionary outlays severely crimps the fiscal space available for other priorities—including any prospective bailout of subnational pension systems.

### Crowding Out Discretionary Capacity

The implications for a pension bailout are profound. A federal rescue of state and local plans would likely require hundreds of billions—if not trillions—of dollars in either direct transfers or implicit guarantees. As interest costs consume an ever-larger share of federal revenues, the government’s ability to absorb such liabilities without exacerbating its own debt spiral diminishes. Even under relatively optimistic economic assumptions, the CBO warns that rising debt will “slow economic growth, reduce private investment, and increase vulnerability to higher interest rates.” In a scenario where markets begin to question U.S. sovereign creditworthiness—a risk the CBO explicitly flags—the dollar could lose its privileged reserve-currency status, triggering abrupt interest rate spikes and a fiscal crisis. In such an environment, extending a massive bailout to state and local governments would be politically and economically untenable.

Moreover, the legislative mood increasingly reflects these fiscal realities. Congressional proposals to cap the Exchange Stabilization Fund (ESF), limit its discretionary use, and mandate greater reporting requirements signal a wider appetite for restraining executive-led bailouts.[^2] While the ESF debate centered on foreign interventions, the principle—that unchecked fiscal commitments can jeopardize federal solvency—applies equally to domestic obligations. As lawmakers confront the CBO’s sobering projections, the political tolerance for open-ended contingent liabilities (such as backstopping poorly funded public pensions) is likely to contract further.

### Baseline Probability Shift

In sum, the federal government’s own fiscal trajectory acts as a binding constraint on its willingness and ability to orchestrate a bailout of state and local pension systems. Absent a systemic financial shock that forces emergency action—a scenario the CBO cautions could itself be triggered by the debt path—the baseline probability of a preemptive or voluntary federal rescue before 2030 is substantially lowered by these fiscal realities. Only a crisis severe enough to overwhelm the normal calculus of budget trade-offs would likely overcome the formidable barriers posed by deteriorating federal finances.
